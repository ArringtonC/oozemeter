THE BOTTOM LINE

Households got a little breathing room in June — cheaper gas, cooler inflation —
but the costs that actually hurt didn't move. Housing, credit cards and car
payments are still carrying most of the weight.


26 OUT OF 100

That's June, sealed. Three points lighter than May.

Here's how to read the number. The scale is pegged to history: the calmest month
we can measure reads 10, and the worst month of the 2008 financial crisis reads
90. Everything else falls in between.

26 lands in the second of five bands. We call it Sticky — our word for pressure
that's real but isn't breaking anything. Below 20 is Smooth. Above 40 is
Slippery, and that's where things start genuinely going wrong.

So: June was calmer than six of every ten months since 2003. Not good, exactly.
Just better than most of what we've measured.


THE RELIEF CAME FROM THE TWO PRICES YOU ACTUALLY WATCH

Gas pressure fell further than any other line on the board in June, and
inflation wasn't far behind. Those are the two costs a household meets on
purpose — one at the pump twice a week, one at the register every time.

When the visible prices ease, the month feels lighter. That's most of your three
points right there.


THE EXPENSIVE THINGS DIDN'T MOVE

Most of the score comes from housing, credit cards and car loans — and none of
them improved. Between them they carry about two-thirds of June's weight.

June improved the way a good week improves a hard month. The pump got kinder.
The mortgage did not.

Employment is the quiet one. Unemployment held at 4.2%, low enough that the
heaviest-weighted line in the whole formula barely registers. Recessions are
employment events. This board is not showing one.


WHAT WE DON'T KNOW

Some important household credit data hasn't been updated since January.

Card and auto delinquency only report quarterly, and neither has published since
New Year's Day. Those two lines carry a big share of the score, and their
figures are seven months old — the newest that exist anywhere, but old.

So the honest position is that we can't tell you what happened to household
credit this spring. Nobody can. We'd rather say that than let a confident number
stand in for one we don't have.


WHAT JULY LOOKS LIKE SO FAR

A few numbers have moved since the June seal. At the end of July the pump was
$4.10 a gallon and the 30-year mortgage was 6.66% — both close to where they sat
in June.

Which suggests July probably won't look dramatically different. But we won't
know until the jobs report and CPI land in mid-August, and we're not going to
guess ahead of them.


WE KEEP A SECOND GAUGE, AND WE KEEP IT SEPARATE

When the news says "the economy," it usually means the stock market. Those are
different things, and mixing them up is why headlines so often feel disconnected
from your own bank account.

So we measure markets separately — their own gauge, their own scale, never mixed
into the household number. We call it Ward M.

It reads 37 right now, on the same 0-to-100 scale as the jar. Higher than the
household number, lower than anything that would count as trouble. Oil eased,
market volatility is mild, and credit is easy to get. The one thing worth
watching is that weakness has been spreading across more of the market rather
than staying in one corner.

Why it's worth having at all: the two can disagree, and the gap tells you
something. Markets panicked in 2020 while most households held on. Credit
conditions tightened through 2007 before anyone's paycheck noticed. Keeping the
gauges apart means a market tantrum never quietly inflates the number that's
supposed to describe your kitchen table — and when they do diverge, you can see
it instead of wondering.

On the one month both gauges have measured — June — markets read 37 against the
household 26. Markets are carrying more than kitchens are. That's it. It isn't a
prediction, and nothing about it changes the household number.


The jar updates itself; you just check it. July seals mid-August.

CORRECTIONS

Two things in last week's edition were wrong.

We said market breadth was unchanged. It was never actually measured — our
collector compares each new reading against a file it then overwrites, so it
produced a zero instead of an answer, and we printed the zero as if it meant
something. That bug isn't fixed yet, and until it is we'll say "not measured"
rather than guess.

We reported June's score without saying it used to read 27. On August 1 we
changed our own formula — a seventh input went in and the rest were rebalanced —
and recalculated the whole archive. That moved 180 of 281 months by up to two
points.

We should have led with that. A number that changed because we changed the ruler
is a completely different thing from a number that changed because the economy
did, and you can't tell them apart unless we say which happened.

Last week's edition stays up, unedited. We correct in public or it doesn't count.


THE FINE PRINT

The score is built from public data — BLS, Freddie Mac, the New York Fed, the
EIA and the Chicago Fed — under a published formula, currently version 3.0.0.
Every input, weight and calibration constant is public, so anyone with a free
FRED account can rebuild the number and check us.

Ward M is experimental and its calibration is still provisional. It contributes
nothing to the household score.

One note on wording, because it trips people up: when we say a line's "pressure"
moved 11 points, that's a change inside that one input's own 0-to-100 reading —
it isn't subtracted from the headline score.

We measure. We don't forecast.


WHO WROTE THIS

A language model drafted this edition from a data packet it can't edit and a
score it can't compute. Every figure was checked against that packet, and a
human approves it before it goes anywhere.

That's different from our monthly Specimen Reports, which are generated by
OOZEBOT — a deterministic script, no model involved — and signed that way. We
used to describe ourselves as having only those two categories. That stopped
being true once this edition existed, so the policy page now names all three.
