THE BOTTOM LINE

Households got a little breathing room in June. Gas prices eased, inflation
cooled, and overall financial pressure came down. But the expenses that shape
long-term budgets didn't improve. Housing, credit cards, and car payments are
still carrying most of the weight.


26 OUT OF 100

That's June, sealed. Three points lighter than May.

Here's how to read the number. Our scale is anchored to history. The calmest
month we've measured scores a 10, while the worst month of the 2008 financial
crisis scores a 90. Every other month falls somewhere in between.

A score of 26 lands in the second of five bands. We call it Sticky. It's the
point where financial pressure is noticeable but not overwhelming. Below 20 is
Smooth. Above 40 is Slippery, where households typically begin feeling
meaningful strain.

Put another way, June was calmer than roughly six out of every ten months since
2003. That's not the same as saying conditions are easy. It means households are
facing less pressure than they have during most of the past two decades.


THE RELIEF CAME FROM THE TWO PRICES YOU ACTUALLY WATCH

Gas pressure dropped more than any other part of the score in June, with
inflation close behind. Those are the prices people notice most, because
they're part of everyday life. You see them every time you stop at the pump or
check out at the grocery store.

When those visible costs ease, the month naturally feels a little lighter.
That's where most of June's three-point improvement came from.


THE EXPENSIVE THINGS DIDN'T MOVE

The biggest contributors to the score are still housing, credit cards, and auto
loans, and none of them meaningfully improved. Together those three categories
account for about two-thirds of June's total weight.

June improved the way a good week improves a difficult month. Filling up the car
became a little less painful. Paying the mortgage did not.

Employment continues to be the quietest part of the index. Unemployment held at
4.2%, low enough that the heaviest-weighted input in the formula remains
relatively calm. Recessions are typically employment events, and this month's
data doesn't point in that direction.


WHAT WE DON'T KNOW

Some of the most important household credit data is still missing.

Credit card and auto loan delinquency figures are only published quarterly, and
neither series has been updated since January. Those metrics carry meaningful
weight in the score, so the newest available data is now seven months old.

The honest answer is that the official national series can't yet tell us what
happened to household credit this spring. We'd rather acknowledge that
uncertainty than pretend newer numbers exist.


WHAT JULY LOOKS LIKE SO FAR

A few indicators have moved since June was finalized. By the end of July, gas
averaged about $4.10 per gallon while the 30-year mortgage rate sat at 6.66%,
both close to where they finished June.

That suggests July probably won't look dramatically different. But we won't know
for sure until the July jobs report and inflation data arrive in mid-August, and
we're not interested in guessing before the data does.


WE KEEP A SECOND GAUGE, AND WE KEEP IT SEPARATE

When headlines talk about "the economy," they're often talking about the stock
market. Those aren't the same thing, and confusing the two is one reason
economic news can feel disconnected from everyday life.

That's why we track markets separately. They have their own gauge, their own
scale, and they never feed into the household score. We call that measure Ward M.

Ward M also runs 0 to 100, but it's calibrated independently, so the two numbers
shouldn't be treated as interchangeable. It currently reads 37 on July evidence.
Oil prices have eased, market volatility remains relatively low, and credit
conditions are still accommodative. The main trend we're watching is that market
weakness has become more widespread instead of staying concentrated in a few
sectors.

Keeping the gauges separate lets markets and households move on different
timelines without one distorting the other. Our published research on that split
is public, and it's the reason the two were never combined into a single number.

One comparison worth making carefully. The household score is sealed at June,
while Ward M's current reading uses July data, so those two figures aren't
measuring the same window. For the last month both gauges fully share — June —
Ward M read 30 against the household 26. Markets were carrying more pressure
than households were. That's an observation, not a prediction, and it doesn't
change the household score.

CORRECTIONS

Two things in last week's edition were wrong.

We said market breadth was unchanged. In reality, it wasn't measured at all. A
bug in our data collector compared each reading against a file it immediately
overwrote, producing a zero instead of a valid measurement, and we published
that zero as though it meant something. Until the bug is fixed, we'll report
that market breadth was not measured.

We also reported June's score without explaining that it previously read 27. On
August 1 we released version 3.0.0 of our formula, adding a seventh input and
rebalancing the weights across the model. After recalculating the historical
archive, 180 of 281 monthly scores changed by as much as two points.

We should have made that clear from the beginning. A number that changes because
we updated the formula is fundamentally different from a number that changes
because the economy changed. Readers deserve to know which one happened.

Last week's edition remains online exactly as published. We correct mistakes
publicly or they don't count.


THE FINE PRINT

The score is built from public data provided by the BLS, Freddie Mac, the New
York Fed, the EIA, and the Chicago Fed, using our published version 3.0.0
methodology. The sources, inputs, weights, and calibration rules are all
published, so an independent reader can reproduce the score and check us.

Ward M remains experimental and its calibration is still provisional. It does
not contribute to the household score.

One clarification that often causes confusion. When we say an individual input's
pressure moved by 11 points, we're referring only to that metric's own 0-to-100
scale. It is not an 11-point change to the overall household score.

We measure. We don't forecast.


WHO WROTE THIS

A language model drafted this edition from a data packet it could not edit and a
score it could not calculate. Every figure was verified against that packet
before publication, and a human approves every edition before it goes out.

That's different from our monthly Specimen Reports, which are generated entirely
by OOZEBOT, a deterministic script with no language model involved, and are
signed accordingly. We previously described our publishing process as having only
those two categories. That changed with the introduction of this weekly edition,
so our policy page now documents all three.
