26 OUT OF 100

That's June, sealed. Sticky — the band where normal economies live — and three
points lighter than May.

Another way to hold it: June was calmer than six of every ten months since 2003.
Not good, exactly. Just better than most of what we've measured.


THE RELIEF CAME FROM THE TWO PRICES YOU ACTUALLY WATCH

Gas pressure fell further than any other line on the board in June, and
inflation wasn't far behind. Those are the two costs a household meets on
purpose — one at the pump twice a week, one at the register every time.

When the visible prices ease, the month feels lighter. That's most of your three
points right there.


THE EXPENSIVE THINGS DIDN'T MOVE

Housing and credit-card debt each account for 6 of the month's 26 ounces. Car
loans take another 5. That's 17 of 26 sitting on the three bills nobody
renegotiates in a week, and none of them budged.

So June improved the way a good week improves a hard month. The pump got kinder.
The mortgage did not.

Employment is the quiet one. Unemployment held at 4.2%, and that maps to a low
enough reading that the heaviest-weighted line in the entire formula contributes
just 3 of the 26 ounces — less than gas, and well under any of the three big
bills. Recessions are employment events. This board is not showing one.


ELEVEN OF THOSE OUNCES ARE RUNNING ON JANUARY

Worth knowing before you trust the number too far.

Credit-card and auto delinquency report quarterly, and neither has published
since New Year's Day. Their figures — 2.9% and 7.7% — are the newest that exist
anywhere, and they are seven months old. Together they carry 11 of the month's
26 ounces.

Which means the jar genuinely cannot tell you what happened to household credit
this spring. Nobody can. We'd rather say so than let a confident number stand in
for one we don't have.


WHERE THINGS STAND RIGHT NOW

The seal is June, but a few numbers have moved since. At the end of July the
pump was $4.10 a gallon, and the 30-year mortgage was 6.66%. Those are today's
levels, not June's — they'll feed the July seal, which lands when the jobs
report and CPI do.

One limit, stated plainly: we store month-to-month change, not week-to-week. So
we can tell you where a price stands, but not how far it travelled in the last
seven days. That gets fixed on our side, not yours.


MARKETS ARE JUMPIER THAN KITCHENS

Ward M — the market wing, experimental, and it never enters the number above —
reads 37.

Four of its six gauges cooled: oil at $80 a barrel, the VIX averaging 17.1,
credit conditions loose, the yield curve positive at 0.73pp. The dollar ticked
the other way.

The interesting one is breadth, which measures how much of the market is
weakening at once. It's the hottest gauge on the panel at 50 out of 100 — six
tickers steady, four softening, one stressed. We can't tell you how far it moved
this week, because of a bug in how we store last week's reading. We're not going
to round that to zero and call it flat, which is what we did last week and got
wrong.

Ward 37 against a household 26 is not a fair comparison — the ward is on July
evidence, the jar is sealed at June. On the one month they both cover, June, the
ward read 30 and the jar read 26. Markets were carrying more than kitchens were.

The two instruments are never averaged. They share exactly one input, the Chicago
Fed's financial-conditions index, which carries 3% of the household score. Worth
saying out loud so nobody discovers it and assumes we were hiding it.


WHAT'S NEXT

July seals when the jobs report and CPI land. Ward M's gauges roll when the Fed,
Cboe and EIA series post.

The jar updates itself; you just check it.

CORRECTIONS

Two things in last week's edition were wrong, and both are our fault.

**We said market breadth was unchanged.** It wasn't measured. Our collector
compares each new reading against a file it then overwrites, so it produced a
zero instead of an answer, and we printed the zero as if it meant something.
Breadth is also the reason Ward M reads 37 rather than roughly 28 — so calling
it flat didn't just miss a number, it missed the story. The collector still
isn't fixed. That work is open and listed publicly.

**We reported June as 26 without saying it used to be 27.** On August 1 we
changed our own formula: a seventh line went in, the other six were rescaled,
and the whole archive was recalculated. That moved 180 of 281 months by up to two
points and changed nine band labels. June went from 27 to 26 in the process.

We should have led with that. A number that changed because we changed the ruler
is a different thing from a number that changed because the economy did, and you
can't tell them apart unless we say which happened. The full record is public in
data/revisions.json.

Last week's edition stays up, unedited. We correct in public or it doesn't count.


ABOUT THESE NUMBERS

The Ooze Score summarizes household economic pressure under methodology 3.0.0,
built from public series — BLS, Freddie Mac, the New York Fed, the EIA, and the
Chicago Fed. Ward M measures market and financial-system stress on its own
scale, contributes nothing to the household score, and its calibration remains
provisional.

Two notes on units, because they're easy to mix up. A line's "pressure" moving
11 points is a change inside that line's own 0-to-100 reading — it is not
subtracted from the headline. And "ounces" are how much each line contributes to
the score; the seven weighted lines split June's 26 ounces and sum to exactly 26.

Every figure traces to a cited public series. Anchors, weights and calibration
constants are published and frozen. We measure; we don't forecast.

WHO WROTE THIS
