THE BOTTOM LINE

The household score didn't move this week, because June is still the last
sealed month and July's data isn't complete yet. What did move was the market
side. Ward M fell ten points as the semiconductor selloff we flagged last week
largely reversed, and market weakness stopped spreading.


26 OUT OF 100

That's still June, sealed. The number won't change until July closes.

If you're new here, that's how this works. The household score seals once a
month, when the jobs report and inflation data for that month are both
published. July's unemployment figure has landed at 4.1%. July's inflation
reading hasn't. Until it does, June stands.

Here's how to read the number itself. Our scale is anchored to history. The
calmest month we've measured scores a 10, while the worst month of the 2008
financial crisis scores a 90. Every other month falls somewhere in between.

A score of 26 lands in the second of five bands. We call it Sticky. It's the
point where financial pressure is noticeable but not overwhelming. Below 20 is
Smooth. Above 40 is Slippery, where households typically begin feeling
meaningful strain.

June was calmer than roughly six out of every ten months since 2003. Not easy.
Just better than most of the past two decades.


WHAT'S CHANGED SINCE JUNE CLOSED

The individual inputs keep updating even while the headline holds still, and
three of them have.

Gas came down slightly, averaging $4.08 a gallon in early August against $4.10
at the end of July. The 30-year mortgage went the other way, ticking up to 6.69%
from 6.66%. Unemployment for July came in at 4.1%, down a tenth from June.

None of that is dramatic, and none of it changes the score yet. It does suggest
July will look a lot like June when it finally seals — but we'd rather wait for
the data than guess ahead of it.


WHAT WE STILL DON'T KNOW

Credit card and auto loan delinquency are the two inputs we're least current on,
and that hasn't changed. Both series publish quarterly, and neither has been
updated since January.

Those two carry a meaningful share of the score, so the honest position is that
the official national series still can't tell us what happened to household
credit this spring. We'd rather say that plainly than let stale numbers pass for
fresh ones.


LAST WEEK WE SAID WE COULDN'T MEASURE THIS. THIS WEEK WE CAN.

In last week's edition we corrected ourselves: we'd reported that market breadth
was unchanged, when in fact a bug meant it hadn't been measured at all. We said
we'd report "not measured" until we could do better.

This week we could. Breadth is the part of Ward M that tracks how widely
weakness is spreading across the market rather than sitting in one corner. It
fell 37 points — the largest single move any gauge has made since we started
keeping this one.

That's the whole story on the market side. A month ago, semiconductors were down
more than 17% and the weakness was creeping outward. Today ten of the eleven
market segments we track are steady, semiconductors have recovered most of the
drop, and only one segment is still soft.


WE KEEP A SECOND GAUGE, AND WE KEEP IT SEPARATE

When headlines talk about "the economy," they're often talking about the stock
market. Those aren't the same thing, and confusing the two is one reason
economic news can feel disconnected from everyday life.

That's why we track markets separately. They have their own gauge, their own
scale, and they never feed into the household score. We call that measure Ward M.

Ward M also runs 0 to 100, but it's calibrated independently, so the two numbers
shouldn't be treated as interchangeable. It fell about ten points this week —
the largest weekly drop since we opened it. Market volatility eased, the dollar
softened, and breadth did the heavy lifting. Oil was the one gauge that moved
the other way, up slightly to $82 a barrel.

One caveat on that figure. Five of the six gauges are now reading August data,
but the credit gauge is still on July's monthly average because August isn't
complete. So the composite mixes two months, and we'd rather describe the
direction than pin a number that isn't measuring one clean window.

Keeping the gauges separate lets markets and households move on different
timelines without one distorting the other. It also means a week like this one
reads honestly: markets calmed down considerably, and that tells you nothing
about whether anyone's grocery bill got easier.

One comparison worth making carefully. The household score is sealed at June
while Ward M's reading is current, so those two figures aren't measuring the
same window. For the last month both gauges fully share — June — Ward M read 30
against the household 26.

THE FINE PRINT

The score is built from public data provided by the BLS, Freddie Mac, the New
York Fed, the EIA, and the Chicago Fed, using our published version 3.0.0
methodology. The sources, inputs, weights, and calibration rules are all
published, so an independent reader can reproduce the score and check us.

Ward M remains experimental and its calibration is still provisional. It does
not contribute to the household score.

One clarification that often causes confusion. When we say an individual input's
pressure moved by 11 points, we're referring only to that metric's own 0-to-100
scale. It is not an 11-point change to the overall household score.

We measure. We don't forecast.
