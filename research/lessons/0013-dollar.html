Lesson 13 · DTWEXBGS

💵 Dollar

A broad dollar index covers more trading partners than the familiar DXY basket. Ward M watches rapid appreciation because dollar liabilities and trade invoicing transmit U.S. financial conditions globally.

Tangible win: Explain what this gauge measures, what it cannot prove, and reproduce the exact Ward M input.

1 · Know the measurement

Published input
DTWEXBGS

Year-over-year percent change in the Federal Reserve Board broad nominal U.S. dollar index, which weights currencies by U.S. trade in goods and services.

Release clock
The Federal Reserve Board publishes the index

Ward M retains the observation period and collection time.

2 · Interpret it without overclaiming

Positive year-over-year change means broad appreciation. A sharp rise can tighten global dollar funding and weigh on foreign-currency revenues, commodities, and emerging-market borrowers. It can also reflect stronger U.S. growth, rate differentials, or safe-haven demand.

Limits:

Dollar strength is not inherently bad for U.S. households; it can lower import prices. Trade weights change, the index is nominal, and year-over-year transformation can obscure fast reversals. The current Fed methodology began in 2019 and supplies pre-2019 values as a historical reconstruction under the revised methodology, not the index vintage observed in 2008. DXY and the Fed broad index are not interchangeable.

3 · Reproduce the data path

  1. Retrieve DTWEXBGS from the Federal Reserve Board or FRED transport.
  2. Average valid daily observations within each calendar month.
  3. Calculate percent change from the same month one year earlier.
  4. Apply the published year-over-year anchors and retain the index definition and vintage.

4 · Compare with 2008

Historical basis:

Historical incident files use the latest data available at retrieval: a current-vintage reconstruction, not the release-time view available in 2008. Revised series can change, and a terminal calendar month may be partial.

The dollar strengthened sharply during the crisis as global borrowers and investors sought dollar liquidity. That move reflected funding stress, even while some U.S. consumers benefited from cheaper imports.
