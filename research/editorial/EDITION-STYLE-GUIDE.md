# OOZEMeter Edition Style Guide — v1

**One page. Frozen 2026-08-04.** Derived from the August 4 edition, which is the
editorial model. The Constitution (`OOZEMETER-EDITORIAL-CONSTITUTION.md`) governs
what is *true*; this governs what is *readable*. Where they conflict, the
Constitution wins and this document gets amended.

**Why it exists:** the remaining gains are no longer in copy, they are in
consistency. A reader should recognise an OOZEMeter report within two paragraphs.
Rewrite old editions to this standard, not by feel.

---

## The reader journey — this order, every edition

A reader asks these questions in this order. Answer them in this order.

1. **What's happening?** → THE BOTTOM LINE
2. **Is that good or bad?** → the score, the scale, the band
3. **Why did it change?** → what moved
4. **What didn't improve?** → what stayed heavy
5. **What don't we know?** → the gaps, stated plainly
6. **What's next?** → current levels, and what we're waiting for
7. **Why is there a second gauge?** → Ward M, introduced not assumed
8. **How do I check you?** → everything under HOUSEKEEPING

Never reinvent this structure. A reader who liked last week should find this
week in the same shape.

---

## The rules

**THE BOTTOM LINE opens every edition.** Two to four sentences a reader could
repeat to their spouse. It states what improved, what didn't, and nothing else.
If a reader stops after this block they still have the story.

**Explain the score where the number appears**, not in the footer. Anchors
(calmest month = 10, worst month of 2008 = 90), the band name, and what the band
means. "Sticky" means nothing on its own and must never appear unexplained.

**Introduce anything with a name.** Ward M, the jar, ounces, a band — the first
mention in every edition carries its explanation. Assume no prior reading. The
test: could someone who has never seen OOZEMeter follow this paragraph?

**Ward M always leads with why, then what.** The familiar tension first — "when
headlines say 'the economy,' they often mean the stock market" — then the reading.
Never state its value before establishing why a second gauge exists.

**Paragraphs run two to four sentences.** One-line paragraphs are for emphasis
only, and no more than two per edition. Most people read this on a phone.

**Technical material lives below HOUSEKEEPING**, under the line *"Everything
below is for people who want to check our work."* That divider gives permission
to stop reading, which is what makes the transparency feel confident rather than
defensive. On the web it folds into a collapsed block; in email it stays a
divider.

**Bold sparingly** — key terms on first use (**Sticky**, **Ward M**) and nothing
else. Bold on every number defeats bold.

**Use the metaphors consistently or not at all.** The jar holds pressure and gets
lighter or heavier. Lines carry weight. A month is sealed. Do not mix in new
figures of speech for variety; repetition is what makes them recognisable.

**End sections with a transition, not a stop.** The last sentence of a section
should hand the reader to the next one.

**Every edition closes the same way:** *"The jar updates itself. You just check
it."* then the next seal date, then HOUSEKEEPING. Same trust markers, same
attribution, every time.

## Non-negotiables carried from the Constitution

- **Corrections are public, in every edition, never a silent edit.** State what
  was wrong, how long it stood, and what changed in the machine.
- **A number that changed because we changed the formula is a different thing
  from a number that changed because the economy changed.** Always say which.
- **Never state a change that was not measured.** "Not measured" is an
  acceptable, publishable answer. A default or placeholder is not a measurement.
- **Never compare two gauges across different windows** without saying so. Name
  the last period both instruments actually share.
- **No forecasting.** "We measure. We don't forecast."
- **Every figure traces to a cited public source.** Claims about history need a
  source or they come out.
- **The byline names the engine that wrote it.** Model-drafted work says so.

## Sentences that belong to us

Use verbatim or not at all. They are what makes a report recognisable.

- "The jar updates itself. You just check it."
- "June improved the way a good week improves a difficult month." *(the
  good-week/hard-month construction, adapted to the month)*
- "We measure. We don't forecast."
- "We correct mistakes publicly or they don't count."
- "Everything below is for people who want to check our work."

---

## Voice, in one line

Calm, plain, a little dry. Numbers carry the drama; adjectives don't. Somewhere
between a good newsletter and a lab notebook — enough personality to be
memorable, never enough to sound like it is trying.
