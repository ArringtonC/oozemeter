# The June 2026 report, five ways

**Purpose:** find the boundary of the OOZEMeter voice by writing the same sealed
month in five publications' styles, then extracting what is and isn't us.
**Facts used (all from data/latest.json + editorial.json, sealed under
methodology v3.0.0):** June 2026 = 26/100, Sticky, down 3 from May's 29. Gas
pressure −11 points (pump $4.10). Inflation −9. Housing heaviest at 6 oz
(30-yr mortgage 6.66%). Credit cards flat (delinquency 2.9%). Auto loans flat
(delinquency 7.7%). Employment calm. Verdict: calmer than 6 of every 10 months
since 2003. Next seal: mid-August, when July's jobs report and CPI land.

---

## 1 · The Morning Brew version

**☕ Good news, your wallet called**

The economy's stress-o-meter (that's the Ooze Score, 0–100, higher = ouch)
just printed a **26** for June — down 3 from May and its chillest reading
since spring.

The heroes: gas and groceries. Pump prices settled around $4.10 (gas stress
−11 pts) and inflation cooled enough to drop 9. When the two prices you see
every week both back off, the whole vibe improves.

The villain, still: housing. Mortgages at 6.66% 😈 keep it the heaviest brick
in the backpack.

**Big picture:** June was calmer than 6 of every 10 months since 2003. Not a
party — a breather. Next reading drops mid-August. Go enjoy the cheap(er) gas.

## 2 · The Axios version

**Economic stress fell in June**

The Ooze Score — a 0–100 composite of U.S. household economic stress — sealed
June at **26, down 3 from May**.

**Why it matters:** It's the calmest reading since spring, driven by the two
costs households encounter most often.

**By the numbers:**
- Gas: stress −11 points; pump price $4.10
- Inflation: −9 points
- Housing: heaviest input at 6 oz; 30-year mortgage 6.66%
- Credit cards and auto loans: flat (2.9% and 7.7% delinquency)

**Reality check:** June was calmer than 6 of every 10 months since 2003 —
relief, not boom times. Housing affordability remains the largest pressure.

**What's next:** July seals mid-August, when the jobs report and CPI land.

## 3 · The Economist version

**A jar half empty**

AMERICANS grumbling about the cost of living received modest vindication of
the opposite kind in June. The Ooze Score, an index that distils household
economic stress into a figure between zero and 100, registered 26 — three
points below May and its most benign showing since spring. The improvement
owed little to policy and much to petrol, which shed eleven points of measured
pressure as pump prices settled near $4.10, and to inflation, which
surrendered nine more.

Housing remains the exception to the good cheer. With 30-year mortgage rates
at 6.66%, it contributes more pressure than any other component — a reminder
that a cooling index and an affordable house are different things. Consumer
credit, often the first casualty of strained budgets, stayed conspicuously
placid: card delinquency of 2.9% would alarm no historian.

By the index's own reckoning, June ranked calmer than six of every ten months
since 2003. Whether Americans feel it is, as ever, another matter.

## 4 · The Morgan Housel version

**The bills you see**

There's a rule of thumb hiding in June's economic data: people judge the
economy by the prices they see most often, not the ones that cost them most.

The Ooze Score — one number for household economic stress — fell 3 points to
26 in June. Nearly all of the relief came from two lines: gas, down 11 points
as the pump settled at $4.10, and inflation, down 9. These are the prices you
meet twice a week, posted in foot-tall numbers, impossible to ignore.

Meanwhile the thing that actually costs households the most barely moved.
Housing — mortgages at 6.66% — stayed the heaviest pressure on the board, as
it has for months. But nobody sees a mortgage rate on a street corner.

So June will *feel* better than it *is*, which is the reverse of 2022, when
everything felt worse than it was. The gap between visible prices and heavy
prices explains most of the arguments people have about the economy. June was
calmer than six of every ten months in two decades. It just depends which
sign you're reading.

## 5 · The OOZEMeter version (the real one)

**Specimen Report — June seals at 26: the jar drains 3 points**

The monthly specimen sealed once the jobs report and CPI landed, and the
reading is the friendliest since spring: 26 out of 100. That is Sticky
territory — the band where normal economies live — and 3 points below May.
June was calmer than 6 of every 10 months since 2003.

The relief came from the two lines everyone feels first. Gas pressure dropped
11 points as pump prices settled around $4.10, and inflation eased 9. When
the first link of the cascade relaxes, the whole chain breathes.

**What's still pressing.** Housing remains the heaviest weight on the board —
6 of the month's 26 ounces, with the 30-year mortgage at 6.66%. Credit cards
and auto loans held flat (delinquency 2.9% and 7.7%), elevated against the
unusually calm pandemic years but not against history. Employment stayed the
calmest line on the board — and recessions are employment events.

**What a household would notice.** June probably felt a little easier than
May. Filling the tank hurt less, grocery prices weren't climbing as fast, and
steady paychecks kept the rest of the bills orderly. Housing remained the
biggest strain, but most budgets ended the month with slightly more breathing
room than they started it.

The next specimen seals in mid-August, when July's jobs report and CPI
complete the month. The jar updates itself; you just check it.

# What feels like us — the extraction

**From Morning Brew, take:** the discipline of *one emotional register per
edition* and the short close with a next-date. **Reject:** the jokes inside
data sentences, the emoji-as-punctuation, "vibe" vocabulary. Our humor lives
in the furniture (form numbers, disclaimers), never in the measurements. A
😈 next to a mortgage rate is a joke at the reader's expense.

**From Axios, take:** the ruthless answer-first ordering — their "Why it
matters" is our verdict line, and ours should always arrive as early. Also the
"Reality check" instinct: we already do this ("relief, not boom times" is a
sentence we'd write). **Reject:** the bullet-first body. Bullets state; they
don't *translate*. The household paragraph cannot be a bullet, and the
cascade's whole point is connected prose — links in a chain, not items in a
list. Bullets stay in keyPoints, where they already live.

**From The Economist, take:** the confidence of subordinate clauses and the
willingness to end on an unresolved truth ("Whether Americans feel it is
another matter" — we'd write that). Their dryness is the nearest cousin to
the facility voice. **Reject:** the anonymous-institution distance and the
$10 vocabulary ("benign showing," "conspicuously placid"). Our neighbor-test
fails those sentences. And they'd never say "the jar," which is exactly why
we would.

**From Housel, take:** the *one governing idea per edition* — his version has
a thesis (visible prices vs. heavy prices) and every paragraph serves it. Our
reports sometimes list when they could argue. One idea, stated early, is the
single best upgrade available to our monthly report. **Reject:** the
timeless-essay detachment from the specific month. He floats; a specimen
report must seal. Our number is the protagonist, not an example.

**The boundary, stated:** OOZEMeter is *Axios's ordering + The Economist's
temperature + Housel's single idea + our own furniture and phrases* — and none
of their vocabularies. The five-second promise governs the open; the household
paragraph is the heart; the number carries all drama; the close names a date.

**Amendments proposed to the Constitution from this exercise:**
1. §6: add — every Specimen Report states its *one governing idea* within the
   first two paragraphs (June's: "the visible prices relaxed; the heavy one
   didn't").
2. §8: add — bullets are for keyPoints and "By the numbers" boxes only; body
   prose never bullets the cascade.
3. §3: confirmed — the Economist-adjacent register is right, but every
   sentence still must pass the neighbor test; when the two conflict, the
   neighbor wins.
